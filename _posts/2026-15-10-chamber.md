# <span style="color:#fff;">Cloud Chamber</span>

Invented in early 1900s by Charles Wilson, cloud chamber is a device used to detect ionising particles.
It works by cooling alcohol vapour to the point where it becomes supersaturated (point where there is more alcohol than
the saturated vapour can hold), but can't condense because of the lack of condensation nuclei. In cloud chamber, 
particle flying through that vapour, leaves a trail of ionised alcohol molecules. That trail is a trigger for
condensation, which results in a cloud forming. 

<br><br>

# <span style="color:#fff;">Particles</span>

<p>
  Different particles leave very different tracks. The animations below are simulations, not footage from my chamber. Tracks appear instantly, drift a little and dissolve after a second or two, like in a real chamber.
</p>

<p style="margin-top: 15px; font-size: 1.7em; font-weight: 600;">
  Alpha
</p>

<p>
  Heavy and strongly ionising: thick, bright, straight tracks, around 4 cm long for the 5.5 MeV alphas of Am-241. They are densest near the end, where the particle loses most of its energy, and then stop abruptly.
</p>

<p style="margin-top: 15px; font-size: 1.7em; font-weight: 600;">
  Electron
</p>

<p>
  Light and weakly ionising: thin, faint, wiggly tracks. Electrons scatter easily, so the path is irregular, with occasional sharp kinks, and slow ones curl up before stopping. Here a magnetic field is applied, which bends the track.
</p>

<p style="margin-top: 15px; font-size: 1.7em; font-weight: 600;">
  Positron
</p>

<p>
  The antimatter twin of the electron: same mass, opposite charge. Without a magnetic field its track looks identical to an electron's. In the same field as above it bends the other way, which is how positrons were first identified in Carl Anderson's 1932 cloud chamber photographs.
</p>
